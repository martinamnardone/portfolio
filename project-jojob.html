UX Case Study · 2026

Rebuilding a carpooling platform around trust and research

A UX audit and redesign of JoJob, a real-time carpooling service for private riders, companies and public bodies. Over two months I ran the research, a WCAG 2.2 accessibility audit and a 20+ screen redesign, then tested it with users.

RoleUX Researcher & UX/UI Designer
Scope20+ screens · desktop & mobile
Timeline2 months · 2026
ToolsMiro, WAVE, Lighthouse, Figma, FigJam
Overview

One homepage for three very different audiences

JoJob Real Time Carpooling is an Italian B Corp, founded in 2014, built around sustainable commuting. It connects three very different audiences: private riders (Carpooler), companies running employee mobility programmes (Aziende), and public bodies with legally-required home-to-work mobility plans (PSCL / Territori). About 90% of its users arrive through their employer, so the company and territory journeys matter most.

The live site tried to serve all three on one confusing, non-responsive homepage, pushed everyone to download the app, and kept the offer buried in long, jargon-heavy pages. The thing that matters most when you share a ride with a stranger — trust and safety — wasn't there. I ran the research, a WCAG 2.2 accessibility audit and a 20+ screen redesign, then tested it with users.

Research

What the research found

Discovery combined a heuristic evaluation, a competitive analysis and first-hand research.

Heuristic evaluation — Nielsen's ten heuristics. I audited the core pages on mobile and desktop against Nielsen's ten heuristics; it brought up clear, recurring problems.

Disorienting & not responsive

It was never clear who each page was for (B2B vs B2C), new side-panels opened on every click with no breadcrumbs or way back, and the mobile version was often hard to read or use.

Inconsistent design system

Cards, buttons and scrolling changed shape, colour and wording from page to page, so nothing felt consistent.

Too much text, too little feedback

Dense sections and unexplained jargon (the PSCL acronym, "Green Impact" vs "Limited Impact"), one contact form for everything, and no confirmation after sending a message.

Competitive analysis — 5 platforms. I compared JoJob with five carpooling and shared-mobility services (BlaBlaCar, Uber Pool, Kinto, Twogo and BePooler) and mapped their features side by side. Among the five, JoJob was the only one with no login, no profile, no on-site booking, no reviews and no app preview — but the only one built end to end for companies and territories.

Survey & interviews. An 18-question survey (4 socio-demographic, 5 on commuting habits, 9 on features) reached 23 respondents; three completed it in person as short qualitative interviews to hear the "why" behind their answers.

23 survey respondents (65% women, mostly 25–34), plus 3 qualitative interviews.

Accessibility

A WCAG 2.2 audit of five core pages

I audited five core pages (Homepage, Carpooler, Aziende, PSCL, Contatti) with Lighthouse, WAVE and the WebAIM contrast checker. The homepage alone returned 130 contrast errors and an AIM score of 1.7 / 10. I also wrote three accessibility personas — one with dyslexia, one with essential tremor (a motor disability) and one with moderate low vision — and walked their journeys to see where contrast, navigation and jargon broke down, so accessibility shaped the redesign from the start.

Reduce cognitive load

Shorter, direct copy; a real heading hierarchy (H1–H3); key messages and actions surfaced higher up the page; a "reading mode" for long, dense sections.

Operative accessibility

Touch targets of at least 44×44px, a clear primary/secondary CTA hierarchy, and accessible forms with persistent labels, inline errors and post-send confirmation.

Comprehension & inclusivity

Corrected contrast (4.5:1 body / 3:1 large text), a contextual glossary for acronyms (ESG, PSCL…), short explanations beside complex concepts, and meaningful alt text on logos, badges and icons.

Opportunities

Five opportunities for the redesign

01

Segment the three audiences on the homepage — so Carpooler, Azienda and Territorio are clear within seconds of landing, each with its own teaser and path.

02

Add an account area and on-site booking — the survey ranked profile + reviews as the single most wanted feature; booking and offering a ride shouldn't require the app.

03

Reorganise sections by priority — concise, scannable content in a logical order, with a guided path for each of the three targets.

04

Make content accessible — forms with feedback, 44×44 touch targets, a contextual glossary and text alternatives throughout.

05

Design for safety and trust to reduce drop-off — verified profiles, reviews, company and territory testimonials, an app preview and transparent ride data.

Redesign

A reworked structure and a design system

Reworked information architecture. The old site spread the offer across overlapping pages and side-panels. I rebuilt the sitemap around the three audiences and what each one comes to do — book a ride on-site, compare company packages, understand PSCL — plus an account area for trips, messages and reviews.

Mid-fidelity wireframes & wireflows. Two wireframe sets — desktop and mobile — with a wireflow for each of the three audiences, translating the new architecture into layout before any visual design.

Design system & 20+ hi-fi screens. On the new architecture I built a shared design system — a display face paired with a readable body face, a new color palette and components with proper states — then designed 20+ high-fidelity screens for desktop and mobile. Each audience gets a clear entry point and a guided path, with trust signals and key numbers up front.

Testing

What usability testing showed

Moderated usability test + first-click on the navigable hi-fi mobile prototype, with 3 participants — one per audience, recruited against the persona criteria. I tested on mobile because that is where a real-time carpooling service is actually used, and where readability, tap targets and forms are hardest to get right. Participants thought aloud, and for every task I logged the first click.

Carpooler · in person

Find and book a ride home-to-work, then find how safety is guaranteed.

Aziende · video call

Find the company section, compare packages, and send the contact form.

Territori · video call

Understand the PSCL section and request a consultation for the area.

3 / 3 found their audience's entry point in the homepage on the first click.
2 / 3 completed every task unaided; the third succeeded with light help, slowed only by the PSCL microcopy.
SEQ 6 average score on the 1–7 Single Ease Question — how easy each task felt.

I mapped every session on Miro: what worked, where it rubbed, and what to improve.

What worked
  • 100% reached their main section on the first try.
  • The "women-only rides" option drew real enthusiasm for safety.
  • Direct B2B contact forms made sending an enquiry easier.
  • Visible times, prices and pick-up points made the service usable straight from the site.
Friction points
  • The contact form / CTA on the company and PSCL pages wasn't immediately visible — it needed an unexpected scroll.
  • The Carpoolers page lacked a direct ride-search box, sending people back to the home.
  • PSCL microcopy slowed one participant.
Opportunities
  • Optimise pick-up point and destination selection.
  • Reposition social proof higher.
  • Consider a third "Custom / Enterprise" package for larger organisations.
  • App promotion needs more data before acting.

Prioritised layout changes taken into the next design iteration on the mobile prototype:

01

Contact form & CTA — make the form more visible, or embed it directly in the main page (to be validated with a future A/B test on conversion).

02

Carpooler page — put the ride-search box directly on the page, removing needless steps.

03

Social proof (companies & territories) — move testimonials up, right under the hero, so trust lands sooner.

04

Corporate packages — add a third "Custom / Enterprise" card for wider, more customisable needs.

Next steps

What comes next

This was a first check on the redesign. Next: an A/B test on the embedded contact form to measure conversion, direct B2B/B2G validation with real mobility managers, and more sessions on the flows the first test raised problems with.
