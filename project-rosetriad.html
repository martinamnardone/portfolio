Overview

One unified practice for support, recovery, and inclusion

Amo founded Rose Triad out of two worlds — sport, where body, performance and mental health are inseparable from daily life, and queer community work in Ghent. The thesis: support, recovery and inclusion aren't three services but one practice, held under three pillars — psychology, body, identity. The market, by contrast, was fragmented across separate specialists, while organisations kept asking for structure: policy, frameworks, real inclusivity. Some things mattered from the start and shaped every choice: no before/afters, no photos or testimonials, and language that could never be performative. So the site had to build trust and explain a bespoke journey for two audiences with real clarity, while staying light on text and without leaning on imagery.

Psychological support

Support grounded in clinical training and experience — beyond a diagnosis or a short-term fix.

Embodied recovery

Reconnecting movement, nutrition and recovery without making the body a performance to measure.

Inclusion practice

Intersectional and socially engaged care, where identity is integrated in your health.

Research

Understanding the practice and its market positioning

Founder interview

I interviewed Amo to understand the practice from his point of view: what it stands for, the two groups of people it serves, and the things that had to be respected — keeping client stories private, and a tone that feels genuine rather than promotional.

Competitive & marketing audit

I looked at how 13 comparable practices present themselves — 8 online references plus 5 local Ghent practices (gyms, trainers, coaches, psychologists) — comparing pricing clarity, tone, how easy it was to tell who they were for, and whether they offered more than one language.

Heuristic evaluation

I audited the existing site against usability heuristics and WCAG, finding clear opportunities: make it clear who the site is for; lower the commitment of the first step; bring clarity to services and pricing; make the community visible as trust; lighten the reading load; and fix consistency and accessibility basics.

Redesign

Nobody arrives here casually — so every choice had to lower the cost of asking

The practice offers one safe, inclusive place where care isn't split across specialists, and gives organisations the structure and direction they're looking for. Before anyone gets in touch, they need to recognise the practice, know who runs it and see that it's real — then understand what the work involves, what it costs, and in which language they can do it.

Is this for me?

The homepage introduces the practice and its three pillars first, then splits into two routes. Individuals and organisations need different content, so each gets its own section, but both stay under the same pillars: the practice is one, the entry points are two.

Can I trust this practice?

Social proof here isn't based on testimonials or personal journeys, which are too personal to publish. It's based on a concrete and verifiable community: a network of professionals, associations and organisations working toward the same goal. That's why the homepage includes a community section, linked straight to the practice's and its partners' channels.

Who would I be talking to?

With no testimonials or photos to lean on, credibility had to come from something real. The About page carries this weight. It leads with Amo's background and way of working: years in artistic gymnastics, then community work in queer organisations in Ghent. People know who they would be talking to before they write.

What would actually happen — and what would it cost?

Each audience sees the shape of the work before committing to anything. Individuals get three steps — a free introductory session, a plan built together, then ongoing care — while organisations get three tracks: sport and mental well-being, inclusion in care services, and coach-the-trainer.

Recovery and consultancy work don't fit fixed packages, which the practice deliberately doesn't offer, so instead of a price list each section states where pricing starts, and the first session is always free — lowering the barrier and letting both audiences decide without pressure.

Which language do I have to use?

Dutch is the local language in Ghent and English is the choice for anyone who doesn't speak it, or who finds English more neutral and easier to express themselves in. Both run all the way to the contact form, so the language is never something to settle for at the moment someone reaches out.

Outcome

Results and learnings

Before the redesign, new clients came almost entirely by word of mouth. In the first months after launch, six new clients named the website as how they found the practice. After the homepage, the most-visited pages were About and Services (the pages meant to build trust) and from there visitors moved on to contact. The site was found through search, social media and a local directory, with no paid promotion.

With no photos, testimonials or examples to show, the work had to be carried by clarity. Being specific about what happens first and where pricing starts did more for trust than any visual would have. The constraints weren't obstacles to work around but they defined what the site had to be. Most of the design came down to removing reasons not to get in touch: not recognising yourself on the page, not knowing what the work involves, not knowing what it costs, or having to write in a language you'd rather not use.

As my first project running the whole process alone, the biggest lesson wasn't visual: most of the work was translation — turning Amo's experience and values into structure, hierarchy and words.
